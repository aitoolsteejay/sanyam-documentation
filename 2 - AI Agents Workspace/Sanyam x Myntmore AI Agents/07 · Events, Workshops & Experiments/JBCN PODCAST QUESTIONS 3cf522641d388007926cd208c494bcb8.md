Opening: Set the context

1. We’re at a moment where AI is changing almost every part of our lives. But if you had to strip away all the hype, what do you think AI is fundamentally changing about the way we learn and work?

**SK:** We can maybe convey that **the cost and time of learning something new has collapsed.** Something everyone in the room can picture, if someone today wants to learn how to cook a new cuisine, edit a video, or even understand a stock market concept, they used to need a course, a book, weeks of trial and error. 

But now? They can simply ask AI to teach them step by step, and by evening they're doing a basic version of it themselves! That's the real shift, it's not that AI is smarter than a book, it's that the time between *"I don't know this"* and *"I can do a basic version of this"* has shrunk from weeks to hours.

That changes what's worth spending years learning versus what can be picked up quickly when needed.

SG: For a long time, the hard part was just getting information, like finding the right teacher or the right book. AI has made information easy to get. So now the hard part is different: knowing what to ask, and knowing whether to trust what you get back. That's the real shift.

1. For parents and educators, there is often a very real fear: “If AI can do so much for my child, what will my child need to learn?” What would you say to that?

SG: Think about calculators. They didn't stop kids from learning maths, they just meant kids no longer needed to do long division by hand. Kids still needed to understand numbers. AI is similar, just bigger. Children will still need to think clearly and ask good questions. That matters more now, not less. 

**SK:** Same as what Sanyam suggested. It’s a good example. 

Try something like, "See, we've heard this fear before, right? When calculators came, every parent said the same thing, 'ab bachche maths bhool jayenge.' But that didn't happen. We just stopped making kids do endless calculations by hand, and started actually asking them, 'do you understand why this is the answer?' And that's the real shift, once you take away the boring, repetitive part, kids don't learn less, they finally get time to think more. They start asking 'does this even make sense?' instead of just solving for the sake of solving. 

Now, that's not a smaller skill, that's actually the bigger one! That's judgment, that's reasoning. AI is giving us that same opening again. 

Less time stuck doing the grind, more time to actually build the thinking behind it. So I don't think the real question is 'what will my child stop learning', I think it's 'what will my child finally get the time to learn properly.'”

1. Is the bigger question really what AI can do—or what we choose to do with it?

**SK:** Honestly, I think we're asking the wrong question when we ask 'what can AI do.' It can write an essay, plan a trip, draft an email, sure.

But give the same AI tool to two different people, and you'll get two completely different outcomes. One person uses it to write a lazy, copy-paste assignment. Another uses the exact same tool to understand a topic so well they can explain it in their own words.

Same tool, same power, completely different result. That's not the AI's decision, that's the human's.

So the real question was never 'what can this thing do', it's 'what do we actually want, and are we using it to get there, or just using it because it's there.'

SG: It's what we choose to do with it. AI keeps getting more powerful whether we like it or not. The real question is what we, as people, decide to use it for. A tool always reflects the person using it, and AI is no different.

### AI & Children

1. Children are naturally curious, but as adults we often tell them what to think rather than helping them learn how to think. Can AI actually make children better thinkers, or does it risk doing the opposite?

**SK:** This is kinda tricky. People with other strong, and relevant BG’s would be present in the room for this question. So, I guess this can be avoided as it's more of a child-development question, better suited to an educator on stage than something you would have a strong personal answer to.

SG: Both are possible. It depends on how it's used. If a child just copies the answer AI gives, that's bad, it stops them thinking. But if AI asks the child questions back, and pushes them to explain their own thinking, it can actually help. The tool itself isn't good or bad, it's the habit we build around it.

1. If a child asks AI for the answer to a homework problem, what's the difference between using AI as a shortcut and using it as a thinking partner?

**SK:** Again, tricky. But, try something like, "Here's the honest truth, most kids today aren't asking AI to teach them, they're asking it to finish their homework faster. That's not a technology problem, that's an incentive problem, we've built a system where getting the answer matters more than understanding it, so of course kids take the shortcut, we would too."

"The shift happens when we change what we're asking for. If a parent tells their child, 'don't ask AI for the answer, ask it to quiz you instead,' the exact same tool suddenly starts building the child's thinking instead of replacing it. It's not really an AI setting, it's a house rule. And that one house rule is probably the single biggest thing separating a kid who's using AI to get smarter from a kid who's using it to disappear faster."

SG: A shortcut skips the thinking. A thinking partner keeps it going. If a child copies an answer, that's a shortcut. If AI instead asks, "what have you tried so far?" that keeps the child thinking. Same tool, very different result.

1. Could you show us what a “good” interaction between a child and AI might actually look like?

**SK:** There are actually tools built specifically for this now. Khan Academy has one called Khanmigo, and if you ask it a maths problem directly, it won't give you the answer, it'll ask you a question back, 'okay, what's the first step you think you should take here?' It keeps nudging, not solving. That's the model worth pointing parents toward, not 'don't let your child use AI for homework,' but 'make sure the AI they're using is designed to ask questions, not just hand out answers.' That one distinction changes everything. BTW, good enough to be merged/answer the above questions as well!

SG: A very good example of a good interaction between a child and AI can be using NotebookLM, a tool by Google, and dumping your study resources on it, NotebookLM can then generate flashcards and quizzes.

1. What should parents be teaching children about AI that has nothing to do with how to use the technology itself?

**SK:** *Genuinely can’t think of anything for this one :(*

SG: How to tell if something is actually right, and whether it matters to them if it's right. That's not a tech skill, it's a life skill. It mattered before AI too, it's just more urgent now.

For example, say a child asks AI why dinosaurs went extinct, and it gives a confident, detailed answer. A child who's learned this skill won't just accept it. They'll ask, "does this match what my textbook says? Would my teacher agree? Does it even matter if this is 100% correct, or is this just for a fun fact?" 1. What are the qualities you believe will become *more* valuable in an AI-powered world—curiosity, empathy, creativity, judgement, resilience? And why?

**SK:** If I had to pick just one, it's judgment. Here's why. Ten years ago, if you wanted five good ideas for something, you needed a smart person in the room, and that took time and money. Today, any child can ask AI and get fifty ideas in thirty seconds. So having ideas isn't rare anymore, that problem is basically solved.

What's rare now is knowing which of those fifty ideas is actually good, which one is worth chasing and which one just sounds impressive. That's judgment, and it's not something AI can hand you, it's something you build slowly, by making choices, getting some wrong, and learning what 'good' actually feels like over time. So if there's one thing I'd want a school to protect and build in a child, it's not more information, it's more chances to practice judgment, to choose, to be wrong, and to learn from that.

SG: Curiosity, because it decides what you even bother asking. Judgment, because AI can give you a hundred options but can't tell you which one is wise. And empathy, because the more routine work gets automated, the more the human, caring parts of us stand out as valuable.

1. If AI can write, draw, code, research and create—what should we stop asking children to memorise?

**SK:** I'd say stop making kids cram stuff they'll forget in a week anyway, like the exact year some treaty was signed, or a formula they just plug numbers into without knowing why it works. Nobody actually uses that once the exam is over. But don't drop things like basic maths, or even just reading regularly, because those aren't really about the number or the story, they're teaching the brain to think fast, focus, and reason clearly. So just ask yourself, is this actually making my child sharper, or is it just something to memorise and forget? Keep the first, let AI take care of the rest.

SG: Stop making kids memorise things they can look up in two seconds and don't really understand anyway, like random dates or formulas they just plug numbers into. Keep memorising things that actually train the brain, like times tables, because knowing them by heart helps you understand numbers better and think faster. So the rule is simple: if memorising something helps you understand things better, keep it. If it's just memorising for the sake of memorising, let it go.

1. Will knowing the right question become more important than knowing the right answer?

**SK:** Honestly, it already has, and here's a simple way to see it. Give the exact same AI tool to two students. One types, 'write my essay on climate change.' The other types, 'ask me three questions that'll help me figure out my own argument on climate change.' Same tool, same thirty seconds, but one of them just outsourced their thinking, and the other one just got smarter."

That's the whole shift in one example. The answer was never the hard part, it's free now. The question is where the real skill lives, and that's exactly what we should be teaching kids to get better at.

SG: I too think it already has. Getting an answer is easy now. Knowing which question actually matters is the hard part.

1. Are we preparing children for a world that no longer exists?

**SK:** Most likey, yes, and I'll give you a simple example. A lot of us grew up being told, get the right answer, fast, and match what the teacher expects, that's what got you a good rank. That made sense back then, because information was hard to get, so the kid who remembered the most usually won."

"But today, every child has the same information as everyone else, it's sitting in their pocket. So the kid who just repeats the same answer as the other thirty in class isn't standing out anymore, they're blending in. What actually helps now is a child who asks a better question, or notices something everyone else missed. That's a different skill than what a lot of us were trained for, and that's really the gap we need to close.

SG: In some ways, yes. A lot of schooling was built for a world that rewarded following instructions and getting the same output as everyone else. That world is changing fast, and the skills that made someone a "good student" back then aren't the same skills that help someone succeed now.

1. Could AI actually force us to rethink what we consider “intelligence”?
