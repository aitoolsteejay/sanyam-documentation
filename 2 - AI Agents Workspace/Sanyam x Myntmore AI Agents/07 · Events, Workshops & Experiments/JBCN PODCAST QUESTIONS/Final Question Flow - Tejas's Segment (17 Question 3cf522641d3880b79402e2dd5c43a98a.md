#### AI & Children

**4. *(Merged)*** If a child asks AI for the answer to a homework problem, what's the difference between using AI as a shortcut and using it as a thinking partner, and could you show us what a "good" interaction between a child and AI might actually look like?

**SK:** Here's the honest truth, most kids today aren't asking AI to teach them, they're asking it to finish their homework faster. That's not a technology problem, that's an incentive problem, we've built a system where getting the answer matters more than understanding it, so of course kids take the shortcut, we would too. The shift happens when we change what we're asking for. If a parent tells their child, 'don't ask AI for the answer, ask it to quiz you instead,' the exact same tool suddenly starts building the child's thinking instead of replacing it. It's not really an AI setting, it's a house rule. There are actually tools built specifically for this now. **Khan Academy has one called Khanmigo,** and if you ask it a maths problem directly, it won't give you the answer, it'll ask you a question back, 'okay, what's the first step you think you should take here?' It keeps nudging, not solving. That's the model worth pointing parents toward, not 'don't let your child use AI for homework,' but 'make sure the AI they're using is designed to ask questions, not just hand out answers.' That one distinction changes everything.

**SG:** A shortcut skips the thinking. A thinking partner keeps it going. If a child copies an answer, that's a shortcut. If AI instead asks, "what have you tried so far?" that keeps the child thinking. Same tool, very different result.

A very good example of a good interaction between a child and AI can be using **NotebookLM**, a tool by Google, and dumping your study resources on it, NotebookLM can then generate flashcards and quizzes.

**12.** What is one quality you believe will become more valuable in an AI-powered world, and why?

**SK:** If I had to pick just one, it's judgment. Here's why. Ten years ago, if you wanted five good ideas for something, you needed a smart person in the room, and that took time and money. Today, any child can ask AI and get fifty ideas in thirty seconds. So having ideas isn't rare anymore, that problem is basically solved. What's rare now is knowing which of those fifty ideas is actually good, which one is worth chasing and which one just sounds impressive. That's judgment, and it's not something AI can hand you, it's something you build slowly, by making choices, getting some wrong, and learning what 'good' actually feels like over time. So if there's one thing I'd want a school to protect and build in a child, it's not more information, it's more chances to practice judgment, to choose, to be wrong, and to learn from that.

**SG:** Curiosity, because it decides what you even bother asking. Judgment, because AI can give you a hundred options but can't tell you which one is wise. And empathy, because the more routine work gets automated, the more the human, caring parts of us stand out as valuable.
